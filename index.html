HOW IT WORKS

Read it early. Move your feet.

What it trains

Picking up the length from the bowler's hand and the first part of the flight — and turning it into the right foot movement before the ball pitches.

  • Blackout (temporal occlusion). At higher levels the screen goes black at or just after release, so you must use early information. Occlusion is a standard way researchers study how batters anticipate (Müller, Abernethy & Farrow, 2006).
  • Real conditions. Real pace, flight times and bounce, club-level accuracy, bowlers taking turns, the real gap between balls in nets. Practice should look like the game (Pinder, Davids, Renshaw & Araújo, 2011).
  • See it, then move. Stand in your stance and make the real step as you press. Reading without moving trains a different skill.
  • Where to look. Eyes on the hand at release; good batters' eyes then jump ahead to where the ball will pitch (Land & McLeod, 2000).
  • About 85% right. The level adjusts after every ball to keep you right about 85% of the time — hard enough to learn, easy enough to keep the read (Wilson et al., 2019).
  • Feedback fades. Full replays early in each stage, then only after mistakes, so you learn to trust your own read (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter, 1984).
  • Mixed, not blocked. Lengths, lines and bowlers are mixed, which helps the skill last and transfer (Shea & Morgan, 1979).
  • Short and often. About 10 minutes a day. Spreading practice out generally helps learning stick better than cramming it into one long session.

What counts as a good read

The right foot (and play or leave), before the ball pitches (+30 ms grace), and after a real look. Pressing sooner than your reaction time + 0.05 s after release can't have used the ball's flight, so it counts as a guess: half marks, no XP. XP comes only from good reads — never from speed or volume.

What a screen can't do

  • No real depth. A flat screen has no stereo vision. Life-size view gets the ball's size and timing right, not its depth.
  • It can't see your feet. You're scored on the decision you press, not your footwork. Use the batting mirror to check head and feet.
  • No bat, no ball, no fear. You don't learn to hit here, and a screen ball can't hurt you. Real nets come first.
  • Simulated bowlers. Real bowlers have their own actions and cues. Here the release cue for length is real but deliberately subtle and noisy.
  • Transfer is not guaranteed. Video-based reading practice improves reading in tests; evidence that it improves real batting is promising but limited. Treat this as one part of your preparation.
  • Timing. Every press is measured from the frame that showed the release, using the press's own timestamp. The screen and controller still add a few milliseconds; the reaction test uses the same path, so they cancel out of your reading time.
